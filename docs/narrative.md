# Cascade — Narrative and Tone

## Setting

Near-future Earth orbit. The exact year is unspecified — close enough to feel familiar, far enough that orbital infrastructure is mundane. There are research stations, communication relay chains, dead satellites from agencies that no longer exist, and entire graveyard orbits where old spacecraft drift in slow ellipses.

Decades of launches have left the lanes cluttered. Not catastrophically — not yet — but visibly. You can see debris trails on the HUD. You can watch a tumbling solar panel from the 1990s slowly shed fragments. The risk is real and cumulative.

## Who the Player Is

You run a small independent cleanup operation. One ship. A license from the relevant orbital authority. A queue of contracts.

You are not a hero. You are not racing against a villain. You are doing skilled, necessary, unglamorous work in a difficult environment. The institutions that hired you — research agencies, sat-comms companies, national space programs — care about the outcome more than they care about you. That's fine. The work matters anyway.

## Tone

**Quiet.** Long stretches of approach, planning the EVA, running the numbers on a deorbit burn. The ship hums. The HUD is calm. Earth turns below.

**Methodical.** Each tool has a right way to use it. Rushing gets debris spinning faster or breaks the tether. Good work is slow work.

**Occasionally tense.** A Kessler cascade event is not a disaster movie. It is a professional emergency: more pieces moving faster, contracts stacking up, fuel running lower than you'd like. You assess. You prioritize. You work.

**Hopeful, without sentimentality.** The orbital ecosystem is fragile and shared. Every piece you clean up is a genuine contribution. The player is not saving the world in a dramatic sense — they are maintaining it, piece by piece, in a way that lets everyone else keep using it.

## The World Beyond the Ship

No factions at war. No megacorporate villain dumping debris deliberately. The situation is the result of collective action — decades of launches, accidents, collisions, and deferred cleanup — not malice. The institutions are bureaucratic and occasionally frustrating to work for, but they are not enemies. Other cleanup operators exist; they are peers, not rivals.

The backstory of individual debris pieces can be interesting: a failed comms satellite, a fragment from a collision between two defunct weather platforms, a tool bag lost on an EVA in 2041. Small, specific, human-scale stories embedded in the debris field. Optional lore for players who look.

## Primary Tone Reference: Planetes

**Planetes** (manga by Makoto Yukimura, 1999–2004; anime adaptation 2003, Sunrise) is the primary reference for Cascade's tone, setting, and crew dynamics.

- Near-future (2075 in source), debris collectors, hard science-fiction
- Unglamorous but meaningful work; the crew is underfunded and undervalued
- Grounded character drama: people argue about money, ambition, loneliness, and what work means
- Art direction: worn interiors, functional suits, mundane orbital infrastructure
- Crew dynamics: a small unit with mismatched personalities who grow on each other despite themselves

Study Planetes for: how to frame debris work as dignified without making it feel epic, how crew relationships carry emotional weight across routine jobs, how the physical environment of a small ship shapes character.

Secondary reference for tone: **Firefly** (Fox, 2002) — crew-as-found-family, the ship as a home, the texture of working-class people doing their best in an indifferent system.

## The Ship: "Toy Box"

Working name borrowed from the Planetes source. The ship is a character.

Between missions, the player moves through it on foot — low gravity, magnetic boots, the hum of life support. There is a contract board terminal near the cockpit, a tool bench in the main hold, and enough space for the crew to have their own corners. The ship is worn. Things break and need fixing. There is a patch on the hull that someone repaired badly and never went back to properly redo.

The name "Toy Box" is provisional. If a better name emerges from crew dialogue or backstory, use it. The ship should feel like it has a history that predates the player.

## Crew

A small crew of 2–3 characters. Exact names, backstories, and dialogue are authored content (leave placeholders in architecture and code). What is fixed:

- Each crewmember has a distinct personality and a personal subplot that develops across the arc
- They comment on jobs the player accepts — not to approve or disapprove, but because they have opinions
- They react to outcomes: a job done cleanly, a close call, a contract declined for unclear reasons
- Relationships can warm or cool based on player choices, but there is no explicit "relationship meter" — change is shown through dialogue, not numbers
- By the climax, each crewmember's subplot should reach a natural beat — resolved, unresolved, or transformed by events

Tone: not a chosen family in the cozy sense. More like people who are stuck together long enough to become honest with each other.

## Story Arc

### Act 1 — Routine Work

The player arrives with a fresh license and a worn ship. Contracts are small: capture three pieces, clear a debris cluster from a relay satellite approach lane. The tools work. The crew is professional and a little guarded. The orbital environment is cluttered but manageable.

This act is about learning the work. The debris backstories are interesting if you look, but they are not ominous. The Kessler events are minor — a burst from a collision two zones over, extra contracts for a day. The session loop establishes itself. Things feel sustainable.

### Act 2 — Escalation

Something is accelerating. Debris fields are denser than the contract briefs suggest. Kessler events are more frequent and take longer to recover from. The institutions that issue contracts are giving mixed signals — some zones are being deprioritized, some are being quietly abandoned.

Crew tensions surface. A subplot comes to a head in a way that forces the player to pay attention. The ship needs repairs that the economy cannot comfortably cover. The work is still meaningful, but the margin for error is shrinking.

### Climax — The Cascade

A major collision — the kind that produces tens of thousands of fragments — triggers a chain reaction across LEO. If it becomes self-sustaining, the orbital lanes are lost. Not forever, but for long enough to matter: communication, weather monitoring, navigation, research — all of it interrupted or destroyed.

The player has to use everything: every tool, every skill earned across the arc, and the crew. What each crewmember contributes depends on how those relationships developed. The cascade is stoppable, but only if the player has been doing the work seriously.

This is not a boss fight. There is no villain. The cascade is the consequence of decades of collective neglect meeting one bad day. The player's job is the same as it always was — just more of it, faster, with higher stakes.

The climax should feel earned, not sudden.

## What to Avoid

- Combat framing of any kind
- A villain responsible for the debris
- Apocalyptic stakes that require the player to "save" anything
- Competitive or zero-sum relationships with other operators
- Tone that makes the work feel heroic rather than skilled and useful
- Crew relationships that resolve too cleanly or too sentimentally
